## Multiplication Becomes Addition · est. 1:43 (target 1:40) · 256 words

### c02_s01 · Napier's trick
_est. 3:03 to 3:35 · 81 words_

Napier's trick comes straight out of the staircase. Write the powers of two in one row, and the number of steps for each of them underneath. To multiply eight by thirty two, look up their steps, three and five. Add them to get eight, and read across the table: eight steps up is two hundred and fifty six, which is the product. Climbing three steps and then five is the same as climbing eight, so the multiplication has become an addition.

### c02_s02 · Every rule from one picture
_est. 3:35 to 4:07 · 78 words_

That is the main rule: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms. The other rules follow from the same picture. ### c03_s02 · Multiplying by sliding
_est. 5:18 to 5:47 · 73 words_

To multiply two by three, slide the upper scale until its one sits above the two on the lower scale. Then find three on the upper scale, and read the number beneath it, which is six. The two lengths have been added end to end, and because each length is a logarithm, adding them multiplies the numbers. Engineers used slide rules like this for centuries, to design bridges, aircraft and the Apollo rockets.

## Why e · est. 2:04 (target 2:00) · 310 words

### c05_s01 · The area under 1/x
_est. 7:53 to 8:27 · 86 words_

Take the curve whose height at each point is one over x, and shade the area beneath it, starting from one. In the sixteen forties, mathematicians studying this curve noticed something about those areas. The area from one to two, added to the area from one to three, gives the area from one to six. Check the numbers: about zero point six nine and one point one zero, which together make one point seven nine. Areas that add when their endpoints multiply behave exactly like logarithms.

### c05_s02 · Stretch and squash
_est. 8:27 to 9:03 · 91 words_

There is a simple reason for this. Take the region from one to two and stretch it sideways by a factor of three, so it now runs from three to six. It becomes three times wider, but out there the curve is three times lower, so the area does not change. So the area from three to six matches the area from one to two, and together with one to three it makes one to six. The area under this curve is a logarithm, and the curve itself chooses its base.

### c05_s03 · e, and doubling time
_est. 9:03 to 9:57 · 133 words_

To find that base, slide the right edge outward until the area is exactly one. The edge stops at two point seven one eight two eight and so on, the number e. This area is the natural logarithm, written l n, and its slope at every point is the height of the first curve. That is why e appears wherever something grows at a rate set by its own size. Suppose an amount grows continuously by seven percent a year. How long until it doubles? It doubles once the rate times the time reaches the natural logarithm of two, about zero point six nine. So the doubling time is zero point six nine divided by zero point zero seven, close to ten years, and that is where the rule of seventy comes from.
