Chapter one

Nine numbers
in a box

A matrix is a rectangle of numbers. Because it is so plain, it can hold almost anything.
1

Size: rows, then columns

A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns is a $2\times 3$ matrix.

2

Every entry has an address

$a_{ij}$ sits in row $i$, column $j$. So $a_{23}$ is row 2, column 3.

3

It can hold anything

Equations, pixels, grades, prices. And, as we'll see, a move.

01 · Nine numbers in a box03
Two things a matrix can hold
A system of equations
$\begin{aligned} x+y+z&=4\\ x-y+2z&=1\\ 2x+y-z&=3\end{aligned}$

Strip away the letters, keep the numbers in front of them:

$\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&-1&2\\2&1&-1\end{bmatrix}$

We solve these in chapter 5.

A picture

Each pixel is one number: its brightness, from $0$ (black) to $255$ (white). This photo is $480\times480$: 230,400 numbers.

The question that drives the video

A table of grades can't rotate anything. So how can nine numbers pick up a whole space and turn it? To answer that, stop reading the numbers and watch what they do.

Chapter two

A machine that
moves space

Feed the machine an arrow and it hands back another arrow. Feed it every arrow at once and the whole grid moves.
The three rules of a linear move
Rule 1

Straight lines stay straight.

Rule 2

Evenly spaced lines stay evenly spaced.

Rule 3

The origin stays put.

A vector is an arrow from the origin, named by its tip: $\color{#ffe94a}{(2,1)}$ means two steps over, one step up.

The gallery of moves

Grey is the grid before, blue is after. The green and red arrows show where $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ land.

Rotate$\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$

Not linear

Bending grid lines into curves, or sliding the whole plane off its centre ($x \mapsto x + 1$). Neither one is a matrix.

Chapter three

The columns are
the whole story

To know where every point goes, follow just two arrows. Where they land is the matrix.
$\begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}a&\color{#fc6255}b\\\color{#83c167}c&\color{#fc6255}d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = x\color{#83c167}\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix} + y\color{#fc6255}\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}$ Scale each column by the matching number of the vector, then add.

Why it works: any arrow is a recipe, $(x, y) = x\,\color{#83c167}{\hat\imath} + y\,\color{#fc6255}{\hat\jmath}$. A linear move keeps grid lines parallel and evenly spaced, so the recipe survives the move.

03 · The columns are the whole story07
Reading a matrix like a map
Worked example
$\begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}3&\color{#fc6255}1\\\color{#83c167}2&\color{#fc6255}2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix} = 2\color{#83c167}\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix} + 1\color{#fc6255}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} = \color{#ffe94a}\begin{bmatrix}7\\6\end{bmatrix}$
$\begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}0&\color{#fc6255}-1\\\color{#83c167}1&\color{#fc6255}0\end{bmatrix}$
Predict, then check

Column 1 says $\hat\imath$ lands pointing up. Column 2 says $\hat\jmath$ lands pointing left.

So the whole plane turns a quarter turn counter-clockwise. You can read the move straight off the columns, without computing anything.

In 3D: three columns
$\begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}2&\color{#fc6255}1&\color{#58c4dd}0\\\color{#83c167}0&\color{#fc6255}1&\color{#58c4dd}1\\\color{#83c167}1&\color{#fc6255}0&\color{#58c4dd}1\end{bmatrix}$

Where $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$ and $\hat k$ land. Nine numbers, three arrows.

In the wild · video games

A 3D character is thousands of corner points. To turn it, every point is multiplied by the same small matrix, fresh for every frame.

Chapter four

One move
after another

Two moves in a row are a single move with its own matrix. Multiplying matrices means doing one move, then the other.
$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}}_{R\text{ (second)}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{S\text{ (first)}} = \begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}0&\color{#fc6255}-1\\\color{#83c167}1&\color{#fc6255}1\end{bmatrix}$ Each column of the product is a column of $S$, pushed through $R$. Read right to left, like $f(g(x))$.
Where the row × column rule comes from

Pushing a column through the second matrix, written out in numbers, is the row-times-column rule: $(AB)_{ij} = \text{row } i \text{ of } A \cdot \text{column } j \text{ of } B$. Nobody invented it to torture students.

04 · One move after another09
Order matters
shear, then rotate$RS=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$
rotate, then shear$SR=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$
$RS \neq SR$

Real objects agree. Give a die a quarter turn forward, then one to the side, and 4 ends up on top. Do it in the other order and you get 2.

In the wild · neural networks

A layer is a matrix, a shift, and a bend:

$\mathbf{x} \;\mapsto\; \tanh(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$

Without the bend, ten layers multiply into one matrix, a single straight move. That can't untangle two spirals (67% at best). With bends, each layer folds space a little, and the spirals come apart (100%).

Chapter five

The moves you
can't undo

The determinant measures how much a move stretches area. When it is zero, space is crushed, and nothing can bring it back.
The inverse: play it backwards
$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&2\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{-1}=\tfrac14\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-2&3\end{bmatrix}$

$A^{-1}A = I$: do the move, then its inverse, and every point comes home.

The determinant: area factor
$\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$

$3\cdot2-1\cdot2 = \color{#f0ac5f}{4}$. A negative sign means the move also flips space over.

05 · The moves you can't undo11
When the determinant is zero

Null space: the points crushed into the origin. Here, the line through $(1,-2)$.

Column space: everywhere the move can reach. Here, the line through $(1,1)$.

Many inputs share one output, so no move can undo it.

You've seen this · shadows

Sunlight flattens a 3D die onto the ground. Every point along one ray lands on the same spot of shadow: the light's direction is the null space. That's why you can't rebuild the die from its shadow.

Solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$: which arrow lands on $\mathbf b$?

2D: $\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix}5\\6\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$

3D: the system from chapter 1 has $\det = 7$, so the three planes meet at one point, $(1, 2, 1)$.

If $\det = 0$, the planes share a whole line of points, or miss each other completely. Either way, you can't just run the move backwards.

Chapter six

The directions
that don't turn

Most arrows get knocked off their line. A few only stretch. Those directions are the skeleton of the move.
$A\color{#b78cf2}{\mathbf v} = \color{#b78cf2}{\lambda}\color{#b78cf2}{\mathbf v}$ $\mathbf v$ is an eigenvector; $\lambda$, its eigenvalue, is how much it stretches.
Our matrix

$A\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\4\end{bmatrix}$, so $\lambda = \color{#b78cf2}4$

$A\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}$, so $\lambda = \color{#b78cf2}1$

Rotations

In 2D, every arrow turns: no eigenvectors. In 3D, every rotation has an axis that stays put, an eigenvector with $\lambda = 1$.

Chapter seven

A rotation, a stretch,
and a rotation

Every matrix, however messy its numbers, turns a circle into an ellipse, in three simple steps.
$A = U\,\color{#5cd0b3}{\Sigma}\,V^{\mathsf T}$ $V^{\mathsf T}$ rotates · $\Sigma$ stretches by the singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$ · $U$ rotates (a turn may include a mirror flip)
1 · Rotate

$-30.1^\circ$

2 · Stretch

$\color{#5cd0b3}{4.131},\ \color{#5cd0b3}{0.968}$

3 · Rotate

$+41.4^\circ$

For our $A = \begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&2\end{bmatrix}$. Check: $4.131 \times 0.968 = 4 = \det A$. It works for every matrix, any shape; in 3D a sphere becomes an ellipsoid.

At a glance

Cheat sheet

Columns
$A = \begin{bmatrix}\color{#83c167}{A\hat\imath} & \color{#fc6255}{A\hat\jmath}\end{bmatrix}$

The columns are where the basis arrows land.

Matrix × vector
$A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = x\,\color{#83c167}{\text{col}_1} + y\,\color{#fc6255}{\text{col}_2}$

Scale the columns, then add.

Matrix × matrix
$AB$: first $B$, then $A$

One move after another. $AB \ne BA$ in general.

Linear
straight · even · origin fixed

The three rules every matrix move obeys.

Determinant
$\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad-bc$

Area (volume) scale factor. Negative: flipped.

Inverse
$A^{-1}A = I$

Exists exactly when $\det A \ne 0$.

Null & column space
$A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ · range of $A$

What gets crushed to $\mathbf 0$ · everywhere the move can reach.

Solving
$A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$

Which input lands on $\mathbf b$? Unique iff $\det A \ne 0$.

Eigenvectors
$A\mathbf v = \lambda \mathbf v$

Directions that only stretch. Repetition drifts toward the biggest $|\lambda|$.

SVD
$A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$

Rotate, stretch, rotate. Keep the big $\sigma$'s to compress.

Answers

Answers

1

$(2, 3)$. It stretches $x$ by 2 and $y$ by 3: a stretch along the axes.

2

$\hat\imath \to (0,-1)$, $\hat\jmath \to (1, 0)$, so $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}$.

3

$\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$: a half turn. Every arrow points the opposite way.

4

$\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\4&3\end{bmatrix}$. The second matrix swaps $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$, so the product is $A$ with its columns swapped.

5

$1\cdot4 - 2\cdot3 = -2$. Areas double, and the negative sign says the plane is flipped over.

6

$(1,1)$ with $\lambda = 3$, and $(1,-1)$ with $\lambda = 1$.

7

No. It moves the origin to $(1, 0)$, breaking rule 3. No matrix can do that.
