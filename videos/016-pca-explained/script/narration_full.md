The idea is older than computers. In nineteen oh one, Karl Pearson looked for the line that passes closest to a cloud of points. ## Data as a Cloud · est. 2:12 (target 2:10) · 309 words

### ch1_s01 · Rows become points

Let's start small. Forty people, and two measurements each: height, and arm span, the distance from fingertip to fingertip with arms stretched out. Each row of the table is one person. ### ch1_s03 · Spread
_est. 3:53 to 4:31 · 89 words_

How spread out is it? Take each person's height, see how far it is from the average height, square that gap, and average the squares. That number is the variance. Squaring makes every gap count as positive, and it makes far points count much more than near ones. We can measure the spread along height, and along arm span. But look along the diagonal. The cloud stretches further that way than along either one. The directions we happened to measure in are not the directions the data cares about.

## The Best Direction · est. 2:27 (target 2:30) · 341 words

### ch2_s01 · Shadows on a line
_est. 5:03 to 5:41 · 84 words_

Back to two dimensions, where we can see everything. Draw a line through the centre, and drop every point straight onto it, at a right angle. Those landing spots are the shadows of the points on this line. Now turn the line. Pointing one way, the shadows bunch up together. Pointing another, they spread far apart. Let's record the spread of the shadows for every angle. The record has one clear peak. That direction keeps the most spread, and it's the first principal component.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch2_s02 · Two ways to say best
_est. 5:41 to 6:23 · 101 words_

There's a second way to pick a best line. Pearson wanted the line that passes closest to the points, with the smallest total of squared gaps. Look at one point. Its distance from the centre is fixed. Its shadow and its gap to the line make a right triangle, with that distance as the long side. By Pythagoras, the shadow squared and the gap squared always add up to the same thing. So when the shadows grow, the gaps must shrink. The line with the most spread is the line that fits closest. Pearson's line and Hotelling's direction are the same.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch2_s03 · Write it down
_est. 6:23 to 6:53 · 70 words_

To compute this, describe the direction with an arrow one unit long. A point's shadow on that line is the dot product of the point with the arrow. Square each shadow and average over all the points, and you get the variance in that direction. So the whole problem fits in one sentence. Among all arrows of length one, find the one that makes this average as big as possible.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch2_s04 · The second direction
_est. 6:53 to 7:30 · 86 words_

One direction rarely tells the whole story. Look for the best direction again, but only among those at a right angle to the first. That's the second principal component. It picks up the spread the first one missed. Turn the grid to line up with these two, and every person gets new coordinates. Along the first axis, people differ in overall size. Along the second, in whether their arms are long or short for their height. Same people, same cloud, but the axes finally mean something.

## The Covariance Matrix · est. 2:22 (target 2:25) · 332 words

### ch3_s01 · From a sum to a matrix
_est. 7:30 to 8:07 · 86 words_

Searching every angle works in two dimensions. In sixty-four, there are far too many directions to try. We need the answer directly. Start from the formula for the variance along an arrow. Write each shadow as a small matrix product, and regroup. The arrow moves to the outside, and everything about the data collects in the middle. That middle part is a single matrix, built once from the data. It's called the covariance matrix. Once we have it, we never need to touch the points again.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch3_s02 · What is inside C
_est. 8:07 to 8:54 · 111 words_

What's inside it? Down the diagonal sit the variances we already met: the spread of height, and the spread of arm span. Off the diagonal sits something new, the covariance. For each person, take how far they are from the average height, and multiply it by how far they are from the average arm span. A tall person with long arms gives a positive product. So does a short person with short arms. Mixed cases give negative ones. Average the products. A large positive covariance says the two measurements tend to rise together. And the matrix is symmetric, because height with arm span is the same as arm span with height.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch3_s03 · C bends space
_est. 8:54 to 9:22 · 65 words_

A matrix is also a motion. Apply the covariance matrix to every arrow on a circle, and the circle stretches into an ellipse, tilted along the cloud. And remember the spread we recorded for every angle? Measure how far each stretched arrow reaches along its own original direction. That's the same record, rebuilt from the matrix alone. The matrix knows the variance in every direction.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch3_s04 · A bigger C
_est. 9:22 to 9:52 · 70 words_

For the digits, the covariance matrix has sixty-four rows and sixty-four columns, one for each pixel. Each entry says how strongly two pixels brighten together. Neighbouring pixels mostly rise and fall together, because a pen stroke is wider than one square. A few pixels near the edges are blank in every scan, so their rows are empty. Somewhere in this grid of numbers is the best view of the digits.

## Eigenvectors · est. 2:00 (target 2:05) · 275 words

### ch4_s01 · Directions that don't turn
_est. 9:52 to 10:24 · 73 words_

Watch what the covariance matrix does to a fan of arrows. Almost every arrow gets stretched and turned off its own line. But two directions only stretch. They stay pointing exactly where they were. Arrows like these are called eigenvectors, from the German word for own. Each one has a number that says how much it's stretched, its eigenvalue. And these two lie right along the long and short axes of our cloud.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch4_s02 · Why the top one wins
_est. 10:24 to 11:02 · 89 words_

Why should the best direction be an eigenvector? Describe any arrow using the two eigenvectors as axes. Its variance becomes a mix of the two eigenvalues, with weights that always add up to one. A mix can never be bigger than its largest ingredient. So the best you can do is put all the weight on the largest eigenvalue. That means pointing straight along its eigenvector. The method of Lagrange multipliers reaches the same place: at the best arrow, the matrix times the arrow points along the arrow itself.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch4_s03 · Eigenvalues are spreads
_est. 11:02 to 11:29 · 65 words_

So each eigenvalue has a plain meaning. It's the variance of the data along its eigenvector. And because the covariance matrix is symmetric, its eigenvectors always meet at right angles. That's why the principal components come out perpendicular, with no extra work. Put it together, and the matrix splits into three simple moves: turn to the eigenvector axes, stretch by the eigenvalues, and turn back.

_silent beat 1.5 s_

### ch4_s04 · The recipe
_est. 11:29 to 11:52 · 48 words_

That's the whole method. Centre the data. Build the covariance matrix. Find its eigenvectors. Sort them by eigenvalue, largest first. Then describe every point by its shadows on the top few. No searching, no guessing. The same steps work for two columns, for sixty-four, or for ten thousand.

### ch5_s02 · How much we keep
_est. 12:22 to 12:56 · 79 words_

How much did we lose? The eigenvalues answer that. Each one is the variance along its direction, and together they add up to the total variance. So each eigenvalue's share of the total is the share of the variation that direction carries. For the digits, the first two directions carry a little more than a quarter. About twenty of them carry nine tenths. And three carry nothing at all: those are the pixels that are blank in every scan.

### ch5_s03 · Rebuilding a digit
_est. 12:56 to 13:30 · 79 words_

Each principal component of the digits is itself a pattern of sixty-four shades, so we can draw it as an image. Every digit is the average digit plus some amount of each pattern. Start from the average, and add the patterns one at a time, largest first. With a few, it's a blur. With ten, you can read it. With twenty, it's hard to tell from the original. So twenty numbers per digit, instead of sixty-four, lose very little.

### ch5_s04 · The map, explained
_est. 13:30 to 14:06 · 82 words_

Now the map from the beginning makes sense. Its two axes are the first two principal components. Each dot is one digit, placed by how much of each pattern it contains. Digits that share strokes contain similar amounts, so they land near each other. In practice, nobody builds the covariance matrix for large data. A related factorization, the singular value decomposition, finds the same directions straight from the table, and more accurately. The answer is the same: the directions of greatest spread.

## Where PCA Breaks · est. 1:32 (target 1:35) · 219 words

### ch6_s01 · Units matter
_est. 14:06 to 14:37 · 76 words_

PCA is simple, and that makes its limits easy to see. Suppose we record height in millimetres, but arm span in metres. The height numbers are now a thousand times bigger than the arm span numbers, so their variance is a million times bigger. The first component points almost straight along height, whatever the people are like. The fix is to put every measurement on the same footing first: divide each one by its own spread.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch6_s02 · Curved data
_est. 14:37 to 15:03 · 63 words_

PCA also only looks for flat views. Roll a sheet of points into a spiral, like a cinnamon roll. Its best flat shadow squashes the layers on top of each other, and points that were far apart along the sheet land side by side. For curved data like this, people use methods that follow the curve, such as kernel PCA, t-SNE and UMAP.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch6_s03 · Spread is not meaning
_est. 15:03 to 15:38 · 80 words_

And the biggest spread is not always the most interesting. Take two groups that are long and thin, lying side by side. Most of the spread runs along their length, so that's where the first component points. Its shadow piles both groups on top of each other. The direction that separates them has very little spread, and PCA ranks it last. PCA finds the view that keeps the most variation. Whether that's the view you need is still your call.
