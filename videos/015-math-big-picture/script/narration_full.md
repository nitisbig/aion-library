## Foundations · est. 3:15 (target 3:00) · 458 words

### ch1_s01 · The tight leash
_est. 2:43 to 3:12 · 71 words_

Picture a dog tied to a post. When it pulls the leash tight, it can stand in lots of places, but not just anywhere. Every spot it can reach with the leash tight is exactly one leash length from the post. Gather up all of those spots, and they make a circle. That shift, from a shape we recognize to a rule we can check, is the first idea of *foundations*.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch1_s02 · A set is a rule
_est. 3:12 to 3:40 · 67 words_

Mathematicians call a collection like this a *set*. A set is defined by a rule, and the rule decides, for every possible point, whether it's in or out. There's no partly in. Change the rule, and you change the set. Let the leash go slack, and the set fills in to a whole disk. The circle is only the points that are *exactly* one leash length away.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch1_s03 · From rule to equation
_est. 3:40 to 4:21 · 100 words_

To make the rule exact, put the post at the center of a grid. Every point on the circle is the corner of a right triangle whose longest side is the leash. Pythagoras turns *one leash length away* into a test you can run on any point's coordinates. Wrap that test in braces, and the circle is written as a set. Georg Cantor started the study of sets in the eighteen seventies, and sets became the ground floor of mathematics. A point belongs to this one *if and only if* it passes the test. The promise runs in both directions.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch1_s04 · Is it on the circle?
_est. 4:21 to 4:51 · 66 words_

Try it with a leash five units long. Which of these points can the dog reach with the leash tight? The first passes the test exactly, so it sits on the circle. The second comes up short, so it's inside. The third overshoots: outside. One test sorts every point in the plane into exactly one of three sets, with nothing left over and nothing counted twice.

_silent beat 1.2 s_

### ch1_s05 · A shape that rolls like a circle
_est. 4:51 to 5:29 · 91 words_

That two-way promise matters. Roll a circle under a flat board, and the board glides along at a constant height. So you might guess the reverse: anything that rolls that smoothly must be a circle. It doesn't have to be. This rounded triangle, built from three arcs, keeps the board just as level. The British fifty pence coin uses the same trick with seven sides, so vending machines can measure its width like a circle's. A statement and its reverse are different claims, and logic is the habit of checking both.

### ch1_s06 · Sets every day
_est. 5:29 to 5:58 · 63 words_

Anyone who shops online uses this. Filter for shoes that are red *and* in your size, and the shop returns the overlap of two sets. Ask for red *or* your size, and it returns everything in either one. Underneath all of math sit definitions like these: rules that say exactly what is in and what is out. Everything else is built on top.

## Quantity · est. 2:53 (target 3:00) · 406 words

### ch2_s01 · One turn of a wheel
_est. 5:58 to 6:29 · 71 words_

How big is a circle? Take a wheel exactly one unit across, paint a dot on its rim, and roll it along a number line for one full turn. Where will the dot touch down? Just past three. That length is the distance around the wheel, and it has a name: *pi*. Every circle, from a coin to a Ferris wheel, measures pi times its own width all the way around.

### ch2_s02 · Hunting for the fraction
_est. 6:29 to 7:03 · 85 words_

So pi is a little more than three. You'd expect some fraction to land on it exactly, if we just find the right one. More than two thousand years ago, Archimedes trapped pi between two fractions by drawing polygons with ninety-six sides. The upper one, twenty-two sevenths, is still used in schools. Around the year four eighty, Zu Chongzhi in China found three hundred fifty-five over one hundred thirteen, right to six decimal places. Zoom in on either one, and there is still a gap.

### ch2_s03 · Kinds of number
_est. 7:03 to 7:27 · 55 words_

Each kind of number answers a need. Counting gives the whole numbers. Owing gives the negatives. Sharing a pizza gives fractions, which mathematicians call *rational* numbers. And fractions are packed tight: between any two sits another, halfway between, and another between those, without end. It looks as if they leave no room for anything else.

### ch2_s04 · The square inside the circle
_est. 7:27 to 8:10 · 100 words_

Fit a square inside a circle of radius one. Its diagonal runs straight across the circle, so it's two units long, and Pythagoras makes each side the square root of two. Is that length a fraction? Suppose it is, in lowest terms. Squaring gives an equation in whole numbers, and it forces the top number to be even. Feed that back in, and the bottom number must be even too. But a fraction in lowest terms can't have both numbers even. The assumption fails, so the square root of two is not a fraction. Numbers like this are called *irrational*.

## Structure · est. 3:22 (target 3:15) · 478 words

### ch3_s01 · What stays the same
_est. 8:51 to 9:20 · 69 words_

Turn a square a little, and you can tell it moved. Turn it a quarter of the way around, and it looks exactly as it did before. Now turn the circle. Turn it by any amount at all, and nothing seems to change. A move that leaves a shape looking the same is called a *symmetry*. The square has four turning symmetries. The circle has one for every angle.

### ch3_s02 · Moves you can combine
_est. 9:20 to 9:50 · 72 words_

Mathematicians study the moves themselves. Think of the dial on a combination lock. Turn it thirty degrees, then forty-five more, and together that's one turn of seventy-five. Any turn can be undone by turning back, and leaving the dial alone counts as a turn of zero. Moves you can combine and undo, including the move that does nothing, form what's called a *group*. Groups are one of the basic objects of *algebra*.

Those six turns, counting the one that does nothing, form a group of their own, the *cyclic group of order six*. A snowflake has the same six turns. ### ch3_s04 · A quarter turn is a number
_est. 10:18 to 10:45 · 62 words_

Turns can be numbers too. Multiplying by minus one flips the number line around: a half turn. So what would a quarter turn be? Call it *i*. Do it twice, and you've made a half turn, the same as multiplying by minus one. That's all the square root of minus one means: a quarter turn, off the line and into the plane.

### ch3_s05 · Any turn at all
_est. 10:45 to 11:16 · 75 words_

Leonhard Euler found a way to write *any* turn as a number. Each one is a power of a single constant, the number e, which we'll meet properly in the chapter on change. As the angle grows, the point sweeps around the circle, with cosine as its shadow along one axis and sine along the other. Keep an eye on e, i and pi. ### ch3_s06 · Multiplying adds angles
_est. 11:16 to 11:41 · 57 words_

Turns written this way are easy to combine. Take a turn of sixty degrees and a turn of thirty. What does multiplying them give? Expanding the brackets, the pieces combine, and everything cancels except i: a turn of ninety degrees. Multiplying turns adds their angles. ### ch3_s07 · Turning in three dimensions
_est. 11:41 to 12:13 · 74 words_

The same idea steers machines. In eighteen forty-three, William Rowan Hamilton found numbers that do for turns in space what i does in the plane, and he carved the rule into a bridge in Dublin. Today these numbers, called quaternions, turn the camera in video games and tell your phone which way is up. For a circle, the moves are turns, and the turns are numbers. Algebra is the study of moves like these.

### ch4_s02 · Stretch, don't tear
_est. 12:42 to 13:10 · 66 words_

Topology is geometry with the measurements thrown away. Stretching, bending and squashing are allowed, as much as you like. Cutting and gluing are not. So the rubber band can become a triangle, or a wobbly blob, or a star. What it can't become is a figure eight, because that means pinching two points together. To a topologist, every one of these loops is the same circle.

### ch4_s03 · Loops that snag
_est. 13:10 to 13:39 · 69 words_

So what does survive a stretch? Holes. Draw a loop on a flat disk, and it can always shrink down to a point. Wrap a loop once around the rubber band, and it snags on the hole, however it's stretched. On the surface of a donut, a loop can snag in two different ways. Loops that can't be shrunk are one of the main ways topologists tell shapes apart.

### ch4_s04 · Corners, edges, faces
_est. 13:39 to 14:10 · 70 words_

Euler found an even simpler test. Build a shape from corners, edges and faces, add up the corners and faces, and take away the edges. Any loop comes out at zero. Fill it in, and the face makes it one. A cube comes out at two. What about the donut? Zero again. Cut any of these into different pieces, or stretch them however you like, and the count never changes.

## Change · est. 3:14 (target 3:15) · 456 words

### ch5_s01 · A ripple
_est. 15:20 to 15:46 · 59 words_

Drop a stone into still water. The ripple spreads as a circle, and its radius grows at a steady pace. You'd expect the area inside to grow steadily too. It doesn't. Every second, the ripple sweeps over more new water than the second before. Asking how fast something is changing, at a single instant, is the question behind *calculus*.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s02 · Zoom until it's straight
_est. 15:46 to 16:15 · 72 words_

A car's speedometer answers that kind of question. It doesn't show your average over the trip, but how fast you're going right now. To make right now precise, zoom in on a smooth curve until it looks like a straight line. The steepness of that line is the *derivative*: the rate of change at a single point. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz built calculus on this idea in the late sixteen hundreds.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s03 · The curve that is its own slope
_est. 16:15 to 16:40 · 59 words_

One curve has a special property: at every point, its steepness matches its height. It grows the way savings do when interest keeps compounding: the more there is, the faster it grows. Its heights are powers of one particular number, about two point seven one eight, called *e*. This is the same e that Euler used to describe turns.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s04 · Growing the radius
_est. 16:40 to 17:08 · 67 words_

Back to the circle. Grow its radius by a tiny amount, and the new area is a thin ring around the edge. Cut the ring and unroll it, and it's almost a rectangle: as long as the circumference, and as thick as the nudge. So the area grows at a rate equal to the length of the edge. The derivative of the circle's area is its circumference.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s05 · A slightly bigger pizza
_est. 17:08 to 17:41 · 79 words_

Try it on a pizza ten centimeters in radius. Stretch the dough until the radius grows by one millimeter. How much more pizza is that? The edge times the nudge says about six point two eight square centimeters. Working it out exactly gives six point three one. The small difference is there because the ring's outer edge is a little longer than its inner edge. Make the nudge ten times smaller, and the difference gets a hundred times smaller.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s06 · Adding up the rings
_est. 17:41 to 18:13 · 75 words_

Calculus also runs the other way. Peel a whole disk into thin rings, unroll each one, and stack them, smallest on top. They make a triangle as tall as the radius, with the circumference as its base. Its area works out to exactly the area of the circle. Adding up infinitely many thin pieces is called *integration*, and it undoes the derivative. Archimedes proved the circle matches this triangle more than two thousand years ago.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch5_s07 · Small changes, added up
_est. 18:13 to 18:34 · 45 words_

Every weather forecast is built this way: small changes in air and water, worked out step by step and added up. For a circle, the rate at which its area grows is its own edge. Change is measured by derivatives and added up by integrals.

## Uncertainty · est. 3:10 (target 3:15) · 450 words

### ch6_s01 · Back to the floor
_est. 18:34 to 18:56 · 50 words_

Back to the floor and the sticks, as promised. One throw is unpredictable. Will this stick cross a crack? There's no way to know, just as there's no way to call a single coin flip. But many throws together follow a pattern, and that pattern is the subject of *probability*.

### ch6_s02 · Many coin flips
_est. 18:56 to 19:24 · 69 words_

Flip a coin again and again, and keep track of the share of heads. Early on, it jumps around. After a thousand flips it hugs one half, and the longer it runs, the closer it stays. Jacob Bernoulli proved this in a book published in seventeen thirteen: averages of many random events settle down. One flip tells you nothing. A million flips land within a whisker of one half.

_silent beat 1 s_

### ch6_s03 · Each throw is a dot
_est. 19:24 to 19:58 · 80 words_

Make the sticks exactly as long as the boards are wide. Then describe each throw with two numbers: how far the middle of the stick lands from the nearest crack, and the angle it makes with the boards. Every throw becomes a dot in a rectangle of possibilities, and every dot is equally likely. The stick crosses a crack exactly when its dot falls under this curve, one arch of a sine wave. So what share of throws should cross?

_silent beat 2.5 s_

### ch6_s04 · Where the circle was hiding
_est. 19:58 to 20:33 · 81 words_

The share of sticks that cross is the share of the rectangle under the arch. Calculus gives the area under one arch of a sine wave: exactly two. The rectangle is a half turn wide, so its area is pi. So the crossing share is two over pi, about sixty-four percent. And that's where the circle was hiding: in the angle. Every direction a stick can point lies on a circle, and pi is what it takes to go halfway around.

### ch6_s05 · Buffon, and a lucky result
_est. 20:33 to 21:00 · 65 words_

The French naturalist Buffon worked this out in the seventeen hundreds. In nineteen-oh-one, Mario Lazzarini reported three thousand four hundred and eight throws that gave pi to six decimal places. That's suspiciously good. A random estimate improves slowly: each extra correct digit takes about a hundred times more throws. His answer was exactly Zu Chongzhi's fraction, and his numbers suggest he chose when to stop.
